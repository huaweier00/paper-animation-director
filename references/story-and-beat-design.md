# Story and beat design

## Intake

Resolve or infer the source story, intended lesson, target platform, aspect ratio, target duration, language, subtitle mode, audio mode, and watermark text. Record assumptions in the manifest instead of hiding them in chat history.

Use these approval gates:

1. story/action table and complete scratch animatic;
2. visual route, reference-only hero frames, and integration benchmark;
3. final measured voice edit plus adjacent-shot sequence layout;
4. canonical character identity anchors and the minimum controlled reference-only model packs justified by layout;
5. hardest benchmark passage’s semantic, spatial, motion, asset, performance, render, and release evidence;
6. limited-WIP shot production plus sequence dailies;
7. final assembly, master, derivative, and delivery proof.

For a social-platform story, also lock the first-three-second promise and the final takeaway before shot production. A strong hook without a completed ending produces attention but not meaning.

For Douyin or comparable feed distribution, insert two editorial gates before formal voice and shot production:

1. approve a non-deliverable 0–3 second hook animatic;
2. approve a complete low-cost scratch animatic with temporary voice, subtitles, key sound events, scene `visual_beats`, and the final save object.

Read `social-premium-production.md` and record the project-level `platform` and `social_contract`. Do not let a technically valid story manifest advance to premium production while `hook_review` or `full_edit_review` is pending, rejected, or undocumented.

## Scene contract

Give every scene:

- one narrative goal;
- one or more observable events;
- one proof frame or proof interval;
- an explicit duration policy;
- a transition reason, not only a transition effect.
- for a social route, `visual_beats` that state the time, visible/audible change, and editorial function of each new piece of audience information;
- `long_hold_rationale` when a gap longer than the normal visual-beat expectation is an intentional take, stillness, silence, or observation rather than missing direction.

Represent each important event as:

```text
cause → action → propagation → result → proof
```

Omit `propagation` only for genuinely instantaneous events. Write `proof` as something a reviewer can observe, not an intention such as “the scene feels cooperative.”

## Pacing policy

- Let measured narration duration set the first timing boundary.
- Introduce new visual information roughly every 1.5–3 seconds.
- Allow a long scene only when it contains multiple internal actions or reactions.
- Start the next voice 0.2–0.6 seconds before the new image fully settles when a J-cut improves continuity.
- Keep normal inter-scene voice gaps around 0.2–0.8 seconds.
- Cut 0.6–1.2 seconds after the scene's final meaningful result unless continuing action or sound earns the hold.
- Do not use slow camera drift, grain, dust, or a breathing texture to justify story duration.
- Audit the first global visual beat and the opening proof against the approved social contract. A long opening explanation cannot pass merely because it contains continuous parallax or narration.

## Action table

Before shot production, make a compact table with scene, goal, cause, action, result, proof, actors, props, critical-prop recognizability requirements, support surfaces, command-state changes, and whether a connected ensemble asset is required. Reject any scene whose proof depends entirely on narration.

Treat a manifest `scene` as a story unit that may remain one production shot or be subdivided for clarity, continuity, rhythm, and performance. Approve sequence layout and each shot's event and `spatial_contract` before listing or generating concrete production assets. Global look-development, identity, model-pack, scale, recurring-prop, and environment-design references may exist earlier when they are marked reference-only and justified by the approved edit; they never become automatic shot media.

## Ending contract

Write the ending before generating the opening. For an educational, historical, or cultural fable, require:

1. **consequence** — show what changed because of the climax;
2. **source/cultural closure** — return to the artifact, place, tradition, or evidence when promised;
3. **transferable lesson** — state what a viewer can carry into present life.

Do not end only with provenance, punishment, or a generic “be kind.” Connect individual choice to the larger principle the story has earned. Keep the final statement concise enough to be remembered and give it sufficient visual hold.

For a save-oriented social episode, declare one concrete `save_object` before production. It may be a decision card, a reusable question set, a sourced visual reference, a compact comparison, or a quote the story has earned. A CTA, series title, bibliography, or generic moral does not count by itself. Approve its wording, visual hierarchy, subtitle interaction, and readable hold in the scratch animatic.

An interaction question may follow in title/description/pinned comment, but it cannot replace the film’s own conclusion.

## Social publishing package

After the final render passes, provide:

- several title options that foreground conflict or choice without fabricated sensationalism;
- a description that names the cultural source accurately, states the interpretive angle, and includes useful topic tags;
- a pinned comment with one concrete question that invites viewers to explain a choice, memory, or principle.

Avoid reducing cultural material to clickbait, falsely labeling a cultural relic as intangible heritage, or posing a question so vague that it yields only “yes/no” replies.

## Do not overfit one story

Do not assume six scenes, three characters, a temple, water, fire, Chinese setting, or a moral. Preserve the production logic while allowing any number of scenes, actors, settings, and action types.
